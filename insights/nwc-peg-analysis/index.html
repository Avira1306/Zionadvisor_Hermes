How the NWC Peg Acts as a Warranty

The working capital peg is not just a valuation adjustment. It is a warranty — and understanding that changes how you negotiate it, how you build it, and how you avoid the post-closing re-trade.

Discuss a deal Working capital peg support

The peg as a warranty

In a cash-free, debt-free sale, the buyer and seller agree a target level of net working capital at signing. At closing, actual NWC is measured against that target using the methodology specified in the purchase agreement. If the actual NWC is below the peg, the purchase price is reduced dollar-for-dollar. This is not a damages claim for breach of warranty; it is a price adjustment agreed in advance, triggered by an objective measurement.

The peg therefore operates as a forward-looking warranty: it sets the standard, defines the measurement, and produces a mechanical outcome. The buyer does not have to prove that the seller did anything wrong. The seller does not have to argue that any shortfall was a misunderstanding. The number either meets the peg or it does not, and the price adjusts accordingly.

That is why the peg is so useful in M&A, and why it is so often the subject of dispute. It is a warranty built into the price, operating at the moment of completion, before the buyer has taken over the business and before the parties have time to negotiate. The buyer gets cash when the working capital is short; the seller loses cash when it is short. Both sides know the rules at signing. Both sides should know the methodology at signing too.

What the peg actually measures

The peg is a measurement of net working capital against an agreed target. The measurement is what determines the outcome. Get the measurement wrong and the warranty is meaningless.

The definition of net working capital must be written down in the purchase agreement before the peg is negotiated. In most deals it includes the same components that appear on the balance sheet: accounts receivable, inventory, and prepaid expenses on the asset side; accounts payable, accrued expenses, accrued payroll, and accrued taxes on the liability side. The agreement must state which items are included and which are excluded, because the range of possible inclusions is wider than the range of obvious ones.

The accounting basis must be specified too. In most deals the NWC is measured on the same basis that the financial statements have been prepared and reviewed to date. The parties should not discover at closing that they had different ideas about how receivables were pencilled, how payables accrued, or how inventory was valued. The methodology must be agreed in writing before the number is disputed.

The measurement date must be agreed. In most deals the measurement is taken as of the completion date, using the books and records as they stand on that date. The peg is set at signing, often based on a trailing twelve-month average or a period-end figure. The relationship between the peg date and the measurement date matters, because the business may move between the two. The purchase agreement should say what happens if the measurement date is different from the peg date, and whether any known movements are adjusted for.

How the purchase price adjustment works

The mechanism is straightforward. At signing, buyer and seller agree on a target NWC level. At closing, actual NWC is measured against that target using the accounting methodology specified in the purchase agreement.

  • If actual NWC is above the peg, the excess is treated as additional purchase price, paid to the seller. Dollar-for-dollar.
  • If actual NWC is below the peg, the shortfall reduces the purchase price, paid by the seller. Dollar-for-dollar.
  • A collar or de minimis threshold is sometimes negotiated — no adjustment unless the difference exceeds a specified dollar amount or percentage, so that trivial timing differences between the peg date and the closing date do not trigger a true-up payment.

The peg keeps the deal fair in both directions. The buyer gets a business with the liquidity it needs to operate. The seller is paid appropriately for the capital they have left behind. The buyer does not have to fund the purchase price and then inject more working capital on the first Monday morning to pay payroll, suppliers, and receivables collections. The seller does not leave behind more working capital than was agreed and expect to be paid for it.

The purchase price adjustment operates at completion, before the parties have had time to negotiate. That is the point. The peg is a warranty that pays out at the moment the buyer takes over the business. The buyer does not have to wait for a damages claim to run its course. The seller does not have to argue that the shortfall was a misunderstanding. The number either meets the peg or it does not, and the price adjusts accordingly.

Where the peg and the warranty diverge

The peg is a mechanical adjustment based on a measurement against an agreed target. A warranty claim is a contractual remedy for breach, requiring proof of a false statement or breach of a promise. The two instruments overlap but are not the same.

A buyer can, of course, pursue both routes simultaneously if the shortfall arises from something the seller did or failed to disclose. The peg gives the buyer a fast, objective adjustment at completion. The warranty route can address items the peg does not reach — hidden liabilities, misstated historical figures, incorrect disclosures, or items that fall outside the definition of NWC but should have been disclosed.

The warranty route is slower and less predictable. It requires proof that the seller made a false statement or breached a contractual promise. It is subject to the limitations in the purchase agreement — knowledge qualifiers, disclosure letter defences, caps, and time bars. The peg does not require proof of fault. The peg just requires proof that the actual NWC was below the agreed target.

A good purchase agreement makes the relationship between the peg and the warranty explicit. The agreement should say whether the peg is intended to capture all material movements in NWC, or whether some movements are left to the warranty route. It should say whether a buyer who takes a peg adjustment can also pursue a warranty claim for the same item. It should say what happens if the peg methodology turns out to have been wrong — whether the parties re-measure, or whether the methodology stands and the buyer must pursue a warranty claim if it was defective.

How to build the peg so it works as a warranty

The peg is only as good as its methodology. The following are the building blocks of a peg that works as a warranty — a peg that produces a fair, defensible outcome at closing, and that does not become a dispute.

Define what counts as NWC

The agreement must state what is in and what is out. Receivables, inventory, prepayments, payables, accrued payroll, accrued expenses, accrued taxes — each included or excluded in writing. The definition should be broad enough to capture the ordinary operating working capital of the business, and narrow enough to exclude items that are not working capital — cash, debt, income taxes payable, and debt-like items that are handled under a separate definition.

Specify the accounting basis

The NWC should be measured on the same basis that the financial statements have been prepared and reviewed to date. The parties should not discover at closing that they had different ideas about how receivables were pencilled, how payables accrued, or how inventory was valued. The methodology must be agreed in writing before the number is disputed.

Agree how seasonality is treated

For a business with material seasonal swings, a trailing twelve-month average is not always the right number. A peg matched to the closing month, or a seasonally adjusted average, is more defensible. The methodology should say how seasonality is treated, and whether the peg should be adjusted for the seasonal position at closing.

Normalise one-time items

Large prepayments, temporary vendor payment delays, or a non-recurring receivable should be normalised out of the peg calculation, with each removal documented. A peg built on unrepresentative months does not reflect what the business needs to operate, and the buyer inherits a business that is short of working capital while the seller thinks they have already left enough behind.

Define the measurement date and formula

The measurement date is usually the completion date. The formula should be written down. The parties should agree what happens in the event of a disagreement — who measures, what evidence is used, what happens if the two sides produce different numbers. A databook with monthly NWC schedules makes this adjustment transparent — both sides see the same history and agree on which months to use.

The warranty vs indemnity distinction

The peg is a warranty built into the price. The indemnity is a separate instrument that covers losses arising from specified events. The two are not the same, and confusing them is one of the common sources of dispute.

The peg is a contractual adjustment based on a measurement against an agreed target. The buyer does not have to prove that the seller did anything wrong. The seller does not have to argue that the shortfall was a misunderstanding. The number either meets the peg or it does not, and the price adjusts accordingly. The peg operates at completion, before the buyer has taken over the business and before the parties have had time to negotiate.

The indemnity is different. The indemnity covers losses arising from specified events — a breach of warranty, a misrepresentation, a hidden liability, a tax liability that should have been disclosed. The indemnity requires proof that the specified event occurred and that the buyer suffered a loss as a result. The indemnity is not a measurement against a target. The indemnity is a remedy for breach.

The distinction matters because the two instruments have different limitations. The peg is limited by the definition of NWC and the methodology agreed at signing. The indemnity is limited by the knowledge qualifiers, disclosure letter defences, caps, and time bars in the purchase agreement. A buyer who takes a peg adjustment may still have a warranty claim if the shortfall arose from something the seller did or failed to disclose. A buyer who takes a warranty claim may not be able to take a peg adjustment if the shortfall falls within the definition of NWC and was measured against the agreed target.

A good purchase agreement makes the relationship between the peg and the warranty explicit. The agreement should say whether the peg is intended to capture all material movements in NWC, or whether some movements are left to the warranty route. It should say whether a buyer who takes a peg adjustment can also pursue a warranty claim for the same item. It should say what happens if the peg methodology turns out to have been wrong — whether the parties re-measure, or whether the methodology stands and the buyer must pursue a warranty claim if it was defective.

How to avoid the post-closing re-trade

The re-trade is what happens when the buyer and seller disagree about the peg at closing. The buyer says actual NWC is $X below the peg; the seller says it is $Y above. Each side has its own measurement, its own definition, and its own view of what should count. The deal closes — and then the parties spend the next six months arguing about whether the price should go up or down.

The re-trade is avoidable. The protections are the same ones that make any warranty enforceable: agree the methodology before the peg is negotiated, document it in writing, and make sure both sides understand it. The following are the specific steps.

  • Agree the methodology at LOI, before the peg is negotiated numerically. Once both sides have agreed in writing what NWC includes, which accounting basis applies, how seasonality is treated, and how actual NWC at closing is measured, the number can be negotiated without reopening the methodology later.
  • Document the definition of NWC in the purchase agreement. The agreement should state what is in and what is out, with enough specificity that the measurement at closing leaves no room for disagreement. The definition should be broad enough to capture the ordinary operating working capital of the business, and narrow enough to exclude items that are not working capital.
  • Specify the accounting basis. The NWC should be measured on the same basis that the financial statements have been prepared and reviewed to date. The parties should not discover at closing that they had different ideas about how receivables were pencilled, how payables accrued, or how inventory was valued.
  • Agree how seasonality is treated. For a business with material seasonal swings, a trailing twelve-month average is not always the right number. A peg matched to the closing month, or a seasonally adjusted average, is more defensible. The methodology should say how seasonality is treated, and whether the peg should be adjusted for the seasonal position at closing.
  • Normalise one-time items in writing. Large prepayments, temporary vendor payment delays, or a non-recurring receivable should be normalised out of the peg calculation, with each removal documented. A peg built on unrepresentative months does not reflect what the business needs to operate.
  • Build the schedules before closing. A databook with monthly NWC schedules makes the methodology transparent to both sides. Both see the same history, agree on which months to use, and can test the measurement before it becomes a dispute.

The re-trade is not a mystery. It is a failure to agree the methodology before the peg is negotiated. Fix that, and the peg works as a warranty: a fair, defensible, mechanical outcome at closing, with no room for dispute.
